Gebhard Leberecht von Blucher was the Prussian field marshal who saved Wellington at Waterloo. He was 72 years old, had been unhorsed and ridden over by French cavalry, and still showed up with 50,000 men to break Napoleon's right flank. The Germans named warships after him.

Two of them. Both sank.

## The first Blucher

SMS Blucher was an armored cruiser, 15,842 tons, twelve 21-cm guns, completed in 1909. She was already obsolete when she launched. The dreadnought revolution had made her class of ship a dead end -- too slow to run from battlecruisers, too weak to fight them.

On January 24, 1915, Rear Admiral Franz von Hipper took his squadron into the North Sea for a raid on British fishing fleets near Dogger Bank. The British knew he was coming. Room 40, the Admiralty's codebreaking unit, had intercepted and decoded the orders. Vice Admiral David Beatty was waiting with five battlecruisers.

Blucher was the slowest and weakest ship in Hipper's line. She could make 25.4 knots. The British battlecruisers could do 28. The math was simple.

Hipper ran. The British chased. Blucher fell behind.

At 09:00, the British opened fire. Lion, Tiger, Princess Royal, New Zealand, and Indomitable poured shells into the German line. Blucher, at the rear, absorbed the worst of it. Hipper made a decision: abandon Blucher to save his battlecruisers.

It worked for the battlecruisers. It did not work for Blucher.

Over the next four hours, Blucher was hit by approximately 70 shells and 7 torpedoes. She capsized at 13:13. British crews from the light cruiser Arethusa began pulling survivors from the water. Then a German Zeppelin appeared and bombed the rescue boats, apparently unable to distinguish its own sailors from the enemy. Arethusa recalled her boats and withdrew.

Between 792 and 1,000 men died. Around 260 survivors were picked up.

## Twenty-five years later

In September 1939, the Kriegsmarine commissioned a new warship named Blucher. She was the second Admiral Hipper-class heavy cruiser: 18,200 tons fully loaded, eight 203mm guns, 32 knots. Modern, powerful, and brand new. She completed sea trials on April 5, 1940.

Four days later, she was on the bottom of the Oslofjord.

## The invasion that went wrong

On April 9, 1940, Germany launched Operation Weserubung, the invasion of Denmark and Norway. Task Group 5, assigned to capture Oslo, steamed up the Oslofjord with Blucher in the lead, carrying 830 soldiers from the 163rd Infantry Division, Gestapo officers, and administrative staff meant to occupy the capital.

The plan assumed Norway wouldn't fight. Specifically, it assumed Oscarsborg Fortress, a 19th-century gun battery on a small island in the Drobak Narrows, 30 kilometers south of Oslo, would not open fire on a modern warship.

The commandant of Oscarsborg Fortress was Colonel Birger Eriksen. He was 65 years old. He had been assigned the post as a pre-retirement station. His garrison was mostly reservists and conscripts. His main guns were three 28-cm Krupp cannons manufactured in 1892. They had names: Moses, Aaron, and Joshua.

And then there were the torpedoes.

## Moses, Aaron, and Joshua

At 04:21 on April 9, Blucher entered the Drobak Narrows at 12 knots, searchlights sweeping the water. Eriksen gave the order to fire. Moses spoke first. The 28-cm shell hit Blucher's aircraft hangar amidships, igniting aviation fuel and stored ammunition. Aaron followed, striking the forward superstructure.

Blucher was now on fire and moving into the narrowest part of the channel. Shore batteries on both sides of the fjord opened up -- 15-cm guns, 57-mm guns, everything that could shoot.

Then came the torpedoes.

The Kopas torpedo battery sat in a concealed position on the western shore, commanded by an officer who had retired in 1927. His name was Andreas Anderssen. He had first served at the battery in 1909. On the night of April 8, he was called back, put on his old uniform, and was rowed across the fjord to his old post.

The torpedoes were Whitehead models, built in Austria-Hungary around 1900. They were 40 years old. They had never been fired in combat. The warheads had been upgraded from 90 kg guncotton to 150 kg TNT in the fall of 1939, which was the most modern thing about them.

Anderssen fired two.

Both hit Blucher below the waterline.

## Seven hours and twenty-three minutes

The combination of 28-cm shells, shore battery fire, and two antique torpedoes was more than Blucher could survive. Fires spread through the ship. The aviation fuel detonated secondary explosions. Flooding was uncontrollable.

At 07:23, Blucher capsized and sank in 64 meters of water. Between 600 and 1,000 men died -- the exact count has never been established. Roughly 1,400 survived, many pulled from the icy fjord by Norwegian civilians. Some 550 survivors were captured by the King's Guard.

The brand-new heavy cruiser had been in commissioned service for six months. She had been in combat for three hours.

## The king's train

Sinking Blucher was not just a military embarrassment for Germany. It was a strategic disaster.

The 12-hour delay caused by the loss of Blucher and the blocking of the Drobak Narrows gave Oslo enough warning. At exactly 07:23 -- the same minute Blucher went under -- a train departed Oslo's central station carrying King Haakon VII, Crown Prince Olav, the Norwegian cabinet, members of parliament, and 50 tons of gold from the national reserve.

The king and government eventually reached northern Norway, then Britain, where they established a government-in-exile that directed the Norwegian resistance for five years. The gold financed it.

A 65-year-old colonel, a retired torpedo officer, guns named after Old Testament prophets, and torpedoes older than some of the conscripts who loaded them. They bought Norway twelve hours. It was enough.

## Two for two

The name Blucher is now 0 for 2 in the German navy. No third ship has been named after the field marshal. It seems the Bundesmarine took the hint.

Blucher lies in the Drobak Narrows at 59 degrees 40'N, 10 degrees 35'E. Oil still leaks from her hull. The Norwegian government monitors the wreck and has designated it a protected war grave. Oscarsborg Fortress is now a hotel and conference center. You can book a room in the building where Colonel Eriksen gave the order to fire.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How was the Blucher sunk in Norway?

The heavy cruiser Blucher was sunk on April 9, 1940, by Oscarsborg Fortress in the Drobak Narrows south of Oslo. A 65-year-old colonel named Birger Eriksen fired three 28-cm Krupp cannons from 1892 — named Moses, Aaron, and Joshua — setting the ship ablaze. Then a retired torpedo officer fired two 40-year-old Whitehead torpedoes that struck below the waterline. Blucher capsized and sank in 64 meters of water.

The full story: See "Moses, Aaron, and Joshua" and "Seven hours and twenty-three minutes" above for the complete battle.

### Did sinking the Blucher save the Norwegian king?

Yes. The 12-hour delay caused by Blucher's sinking and the blocking of the Drobak Narrows gave Oslo enough warning to evacuate. At 07:23 — the exact minute Blucher went under — a train departed Oslo carrying King Haakon VII, Crown Prince Olav, the cabinet, parliament members, and 50 tons of gold.
