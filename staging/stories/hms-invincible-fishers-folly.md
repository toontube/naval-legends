## The Idea

In 1904, Admiral Sir John Fisher became First Sea Lord of the Royal Navy with one obsession: speed kills. Not armor. Not firepower alone. Speed.

He wanted a ship that carried battleship guns on a cruiser hull. Something that could run down any enemy cruiser, blast it apart with 12-inch shells, and disappear before anything bigger showed up to argue. The Admiralty thought he was mad. He built it anyway.

HMS Invincible launched at Armstrong Whitworth on the River Tyne on April 13, 1907. She displaced 17,250 tons, mounted eight 12-inch guns in four twin turrets, and made 25 knots. Her sister ships were Inflexible and Indomitable. Together, they were the world's first battlecruisers.

The idea was simple. The armor was thin -- 6 inches on the belt, compared to 11 or 12 on a proper battleship. Fisher didn't care. His ships would never need to stand and slug it out. They'd use their speed to control the engagement. Fight when the odds were good. Run when they weren't.

It worked. For a while.

## The Falklands

On December 8, 1914, Invincible and Inflexible caught Vice Admiral Maximilian von Spee's squadron off the Falkland Islands. Von Spee had two armored cruisers, Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, plus three light cruisers. He'd just destroyed a British squadron at Coronel. He was feeling confident.

He shouldn't have been. Invincible and Inflexible opened fire at 16,500 yards. The German armored cruisers couldn't reply effectively at that range. They couldn't run -- the British battlecruisers were faster. They couldn't close -- the 12-inch guns would tear them apart before they got near enough for their 8.2-inch guns to matter.

Scharnhorst went down at 4:17 PM. Gneisenau followed at 6:02 PM. Over 2,100 German sailors died. The British lost 10 men.

Fisher's concept was vindicated. Battlecruisers could destroy anything weaker and outrun anything stronger.

The problem was what happened when someone ordered them to do neither.

## Jutland

May 31, 1916. The Grand Fleet and the High Seas Fleet stumbled into each other off the coast of Denmark. It was the largest naval battle of the war. 250 ships. 100,000 men.

Vice Admiral David Beatty commanded the Battlecruiser Fleet. He had six battlecruisers and orders to find the Germans, fix them in place, and wait for Admiral John Jellicoe's dreadnoughts to come up and finish them off. Instead, Beatty charged straight at Vice Admiral Franz Hipper's five battlecruisers.

At 4:00 PM, Indefatigable took hits from Von der Tann. Her magazines detonated. She sank in seconds. 1,017 dead, 2 survivors.

Twenty-six minutes later, Queen Mary took a salvo from Derfflinger. Another magazine explosion. 1,266 dead, 20 survivors.

Beatty turned to his flag captain, Ernle Chatfield, and said: "There seems to be something wrong with our bloody ships today."

There was. The armor was too thin. The ammunition handling procedures were reckless -- flash doors that should have been closed were propped open to speed up the rate of fire. Cordite charges sat exposed in the working chambers. A single penetrating hit could send a flame straight down into the magazine.

Fisher's ships could dish it out. They couldn't take it.

## The Six Survivors

Invincible had been detached from Beatty's force days earlier for gunnery practice with the Grand Fleet. She was flagship of the 3rd Battlecruiser Squadron under Rear Admiral Horace Hood. When Jellicoe's fleet finally arrived, Hood's squadron led the way.

At 6:31 PM, Invincible was hammering the German battlecruiser Derfflinger at 8,000 yards. Her gunnery was excellent. Moments before the end, Admiral Hood called up to the gunnery officer, Commander Hubert Dannreuther, in the foretop: "Your firing is very good. Keep at it as quickly as you can. Every shot is telling."

Then a 12-inch shell from Derfflinger hit Q turret amidships. It penetrated the front armor, blew the roof off, and sent a flash straight down into the magazine.

Invincible broke in half. The detonation was so violent it split the ship at the center. She sank in 90 seconds. Both the bow and stern sections stuck up out of the shallow water like a broken V, the middle resting on the seabed 180 feet below. Passing ships could see both ends protruding above the waves for the rest of the battle.

1,026 men died, including Admiral Hood.

Six survived. Five of them were in the foretop -- the highest point on the ship, launched clear of the wreckage by the explosion. Among them was Commander Dannreuther, the gunnery officer Hood had just praised. The sixth survivor had been standing on top of Q turret itself, at the rangefinder. The blast threw him into the sea.

The destroyer HMS Badger pulled all six from the water.

## What Was Wrong

Three British battlecruisers exploded at Jutland. 3,309 men died. The total British dead for the entire battle was 6,094, meaning more than half came from magazine detonations on three ships.

The Germans hit their own battlecruisers just as hard. SMS Seydlitz took 21 heavy-caliber hits and had both forward turrets destroyed. She shipped 5,300 tons of water. She nearly capsized on the way home. But she didn't explode. German ships had better flash protection between the turret and the magazine -- interlocking doors that couldn't be opened simultaneously.

The British knew about flash risks. After the Battle of Dogger Bank in 1915, HMS Lion nearly blew up from a turret hit. The surviving turret officer, Major Francis Harvey of the Royal Marines, ordered the magazine flooded with his dying breath. He received a posthumous Victoria Cross.

The lesson was noted. New safety procedures were issued. They were not followed. Speed of fire mattered more to the gun crews than safety doors.

Fisher retired before Jutland. He never saw what happened when his fast, thin-skinned ships met an enemy they couldn't avoid. The concept wasn't wrong -- at the Falklands, it worked perfectly. But at Jutland, battlecruisers were thrown into a battleship fight. They were never built for that.

## What Remains

The wreck of HMS Invincible lies in 180 feet of water in the North Sea, 57 degrees 2 minutes north. Both the bow and stern sections are still identifiable. The site is protected under the Protection of Military Remains Act 1986. Diving is prohibited.

Rear Admiral Hood's body was never recovered. His son, also named Horace, survived the Second World War. The battlecruiser HMS Hood -- the ship that blew up fighting Bismarck in 1941 -- was named after his ancestor, Admiral Samuel Hood. The family kept producing sailors. The sea kept taking them.
