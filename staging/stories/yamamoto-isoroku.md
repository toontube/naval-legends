## The Man With Eight Fingers

Yamamoto Isoroku was born Takano Isoroku on April 4, 1884, in Nagaoka, Japan. His father was 56 years old. "Isoroku" means "56" in an old Japanese counting system. He was adopted into the Yamamoto family in 1916 and took their name.

He graduated from the Imperial Japanese Naval Academy in 1904, just in time for the Russo-Japanese War. At the Battle of Tsushima on May 27, 1905, an explosion in his ship's forward turret tore a wound in his thigh "the size of a baby's head" and took the index and middle fingers of his left hand. He was 21. He kept the hand. He lost the fingers.

Geisha in the red-light districts of Tokyo later gave him a nickname: "Eighty Sen." A manicure cost ten sen per finger. Yamamoto only needed eight.

## The American Education

In 1919, Yamamoto arrived at Harvard University to study English and economics. He spent his free time studying oil -- touring American petroleum fields in Texas and Mexico, calculating how much fuel an industrial economy consumed. He played poker obsessively. He was good at it. He understood bluffing, calculated risk, and when to fold.

From 1926 to 1928, he served as naval attache at the Japanese Embassy in Washington, D.C. He traveled across the United States. He visited factories in Detroit, oil refineries in Texas, steel mills in Pittsburgh. He saw what America could build.

Most Japanese military officers in the 1930s had never been to the United States. They saw a soft, decadent nation that would sue for peace after one hard blow. Yamamoto had driven through it. He knew better.

In 1940, as Commander-in-Chief of the Combined Fleet, he wrote to a friend: "Should hostilities once break out between Japan and the United States, it is not enough that we take Guam and the Philippines, nor even Hawaii and San Francisco. We would have to march into Washington and dictate the terms of peace in the White House."

He wasn't boasting. He was saying it was impossible.

## Six Months

When the Imperial Japanese Navy's General Staff pushed for war with America, Yamamoto fought it. He'd been fighting it for years. He'd received death threats from ultranationalists for opposing the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy. The Navy Minister had to post guards outside his house.

But when the decision was made above his head, he did what he was ordered to do. He planned the attack on Pearl Harbor.

The concept was his: a massed carrier strike across 4,000 miles of open ocean to destroy the American Pacific Fleet at anchor. Six carriers. 353 aircraft. December 7, 1941. It worked. Eight battleships hit. 2,403 Americans dead. The Pacific Fleet's battle line was wrecked.

He told Prime Minister Fumimaro Konoe before the attack: "If I am told to fight regardless of the consequences, I shall run wild considerably for the first six months or a year, but I have utterly no confidence for the second and third years."

Pearl Harbor was December 7, 1941. The Battle of Midway was June 4-7, 1942. Six months.

At Midway, Yamamoto lost four fleet carriers -- Akagi, Kaga, Soryu, Hiryu -- in a single afternoon. Japan never replaced them. The industrial capacity he'd warned about kicked into gear. American shipyards launched 24 fleet carriers by the end of the war. Japan launched 5.

## Operation Vengeance

By the spring of 1943, Yamamoto was running a losing campaign in the Solomon Islands. On April 14, American naval intelligence intercepted and decoded a Japanese message detailing his travel itinerary. Admiral Yamamoto would fly from Rabaul to Balalae Island on April 18 for a front-line inspection tour. Departure at 6:00 AM. Arrival at 8:00 AM. Two Mitsubishi G4M "Betty" bombers. Six Zero escorts.

The intercept went to Secretary of the Navy Frank Knox, then to President Franklin Roosevelt. The order came back: get him.

The only American fighter with the range to reach the intercept point was the Lockheed P-38 Lightning. Major John W. Mitchell of the 339th Fighter Squadron planned the mission. Eighteen P-38s from Henderson Field on Guadalcanal. Four in the "killer" flight. Fourteen as top cover. The route was 430 miles over open water, at wave-top height to avoid detection, with no radio communication.

They arrived at the intercept point at 9:34 AM, one minute early. Yamamoto's two bombers appeared descending through light haze, right on schedule.

Captain Thomas Lanphier and First Lieutenant Rex Barber peeled off to attack. What happened next became one of the war's longest-running arguments. Lanphier claimed he shot down the first bomber. Barber claimed the same. Lanphier said he attacked head-on. Barber said he came in from behind and put a long burst into the right engine and fuselage. Barber's P-38 came back with 104 bullet holes. The Air Force eventually gave half credit to each man. The argument lasted until both of them died.

The bomber carrying Yamamoto crashed into the jungle near Buin, Bougainville. A Japanese search party found him the next day, still in his seat, thrown from the wreckage. He was upright, hand on his katana. Two bullet wounds -- one in his left shoulder, one that had entered his lower jaw and exited above his right eye.

He was 59 years old. He had held his rank for four years, four months, and two days.

## The Aftermath

The Americans kept Operation Vengeance secret for weeks to protect their codebreaking capability. They leaked a cover story suggesting Australian coastwatchers had spotted Yamamoto's plane.

Japan announced his death on May 21, 1943 -- five weeks after the shootdown. He received a state funeral on June 5, the largest since Admiral Togo Heihachiro's in 1934. His ashes were carried through Tokyo on a gun carriage.

The six months he'd predicted turned out to be optimistic. From Midway onward, Japan won no major naval engagements. The carriers built by the American factories he'd visited as a young attache arrived in the Pacific in a flood -- Essex, Yorktown, Intrepid, Hornet, Franklin, Bunker Hill, one after another after another.

Yamamoto had seen it coming. He'd told them. He'd written it down. He'd said it to anyone who would listen.

Nobody listened.

His left hand, with its missing fingers from Tsushima in 1905, was identified as one of the ways the Japanese search party confirmed his body in the jungle on Bougainville in 1943. The war he'd warned against had 28 months left to run.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How did Yamamoto die?

Admiral Yamamoto was killed on April 18, 1943, when American P-38 Lightning fighters intercepted his transport bomber over Bougainville in the Solomon Islands. U.S. naval intelligence had decoded a Japanese message detailing his exact flight itinerary. President Roosevelt authorized the mission, codenamed Operation Vengeance. Eighteen P-38s flew 430 miles at wave-top height and shot down his plane. He was 59 years old.

The full story: See "Operation Vengeance" above for the full account of the intercept mission and its aftermath.

### Did Yamamoto oppose the war with America?

Yes. Yamamoto fought against war with the United States for years and received death threats from ultranationalist groups for opposing the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy. Having studied at Harvard and traveled extensively across America, he understood its industrial capacity and warned that Japan could only "run wild" for six months before American production would overwhelm them. He was overruled, and then ordered to plan the attack he had argued against.
