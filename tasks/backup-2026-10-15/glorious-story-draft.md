On the afternoon of 8 June 1940, HMS Glorious — a 22,500-ton fleet carrier — was steaming for Scapa Flow with two destroyers and not a single plane in the air. No fighters on deck. No scouts ahead. The lookouts in her crow's nests were the only thing between her and 1,500 miles of open Norwegian Sea.

She was in that state because her captain was rushing home to a court-martial.

## The captain

Captain Guy D'Oyly-Hughes had been a hero of the First World War — a real one. As second-in-command of the submarine HMS E11 in the Dardanelles in August 1915, he had swum ashore at night carrying a sack of guncotton, hiked into Turkey, blown up a railway viaduct on the Constantinople–Baghdad line, and swum back to a boat that he found half-submerged. He won the DSC for it. He added a DSO and a Bar later in the war. He was 24.

Twenty-four years later, in June 1939, he was given HMS Glorious.

He had never flown a plane. He had never served in a carrier. He had spent most of his career under water.

## The feud

Glorious carried a Commander (Air), J. B. Heath, whose job was to run the air group. Heath was a career aviator. He understood how long it took to range a Swordfish, what the weather minimums were, and why striking land targets from a carrier with no fighter cover was a way to lose your air group.

D'Oyly-Hughes ordered air strikes against German positions in Norway. Heath argued. D'Oyly-Hughes ordered more. Heath argued again. By late May 1940, the captain had had enough. He arrested Commander Heath, put him ashore at Scapa Flow, and set the court-martial for early June.

The charge was insubordination. The evidence was Heath's refusal to plan attacks he believed would kill his men for no gain.

## The detachment

On 8 June, Glorious was screening the evacuation from Narvik under Vice-Admiral Lionel Wells, who was flying his flag in HMS Ark Royal. D'Oyly-Hughes asked permission to detach and steam ahead independently. His stated reason was fuel. His actual reason, given to officers on board and recorded by survivors, was that he needed to be at Scapa Flow for Heath's trial.

Wells granted it. Glorious left the screen with two destroyers — HMS Ardent and HMS Acasta, Commander Charles Glasfurd in the latter — and worked up to a leisurely 17 knots. Half her boilers were cold. The captain went below to his cabin.

## The intercept

At 16:00, lookouts spotted smoke on the horizon. By the time anyone identified the source, the range was already closing. Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, each carrying nine 28-cm guns, had altered course toward them.

Glorious had no Sea Gladiators in the air. The Swordfish in her upper hangar had been parked tight to make room for RAF Hurricanes flown aboard from Norway days earlier. The crews ranging torpedo bombers worked in a hangar packed with extra airframes, and the standby aircraft had no warheads on their torpedoes.

At 16:32, Scharnhorst opened fire at a range of 28,600 metres — 17.8 miles. Six minutes later, an 11-inch shell came through Glorious's upper hangar at a range of roughly 24,200 metres (26,500 yards) and started a fire she could not put out. It is one of the longest-range gunfire hits ever recorded against a moving ship.

## The destroyers

HMS Ardent went first. She laid smoke, fired her torpedoes from short range, and came apart under Scharnhorst's secondary battery. She sank at 17:25. Of her crew, 152 died. One survived.

HMS Acasta came in second. Commander Glasfurd ran behind the smoke and emerged at close range on Scharnhorst's port side. His second salvo of four torpedoes put one into the battleship's starboard quarter at 17:34 — a 12-metre hole abreast the rear turret. 2,500 tonnes of water poured in. Scharnhorst listed five degrees, lost her after turret, lost a propeller shaft, slowed to 20 knots, and turned for Trondheim. 48 Germans died inside.

Acasta was already finished. According to her sole survivor, Leading Seaman C. G. Carter, men in the water called to Glasfurd to jump. The captain waved them off, took a cigarette from a silver case, tapped it twice against the case, lit it, and turned back to watch his ship sink. 160 of his men went with him.

He was recommended for a posthumous Victoria Cross. The Honours and Awards Committee declined. Awarding it, the Secretary noted, would mean reopening the enquiry into why a fleet carrier had been steaming home unescorted in the first place.

## The men in the water

Glorious sank at about 17:40. Around 900 men got into the water. There was a north wind, and the sea was 5°C.

Aircraft from Ark Royal flew search patterns over the next two days. Some passed close over Carley floats and missed them. The Norwegian fishing boat *Borgund*, on passage to the Faroes, found 37 men still alive on 10 June. Two died before reaching Tórshavn.

Three British ships had gone down. 1,519 men were dead — 1,207 from Glorious, 160 from Acasta, 152 from Ardent. The Royal Navy's worst surface loss of the war. 112 of the Glorious dead were Royal Marines.

D'Oyly-Hughes drowned with his ship.

## The cover-up

Commander Heath, still under arrest at Scapa Flow when news of the sinking arrived, was returned to duty. With his captain and the witnesses at the bottom of the Norwegian Sea, the court-martial could not proceed. He retired as a Captain in 1948.

The Admiralty's public account in 1940 described an unlucky interception by superior forces and praised the captain's seamanship. The press was not told that Glorious had been steaming alone for personal reasons, that no fighters were flying CAP, that no scouts were aloft, or that the RAF Hurricanes she had recovered the week before — landed on her deck without arrestor hooks in one of the first such operations of the war — had been struck below into a hangar she had no time to clear.

The truth waited forty years. In May 1980 the official naval historian, Captain Stephen Roskill, published *The Cantankerous Captain of HMS Glorious* in the *Sunday Times*. He had Heath's post-war testimony to the Admiralty. He had letters from officers who had served under D'Oyly-Hughes. He had what the survivors had been telling each other in pubs since 1940.

The official history was never amended.

## What's still there

The wreck of HMS Glorious has never been found. The best estimate puts her north-west of Trondheim in roughly 3,500 metres of water. Acasta and Ardent are down there too.

The names of the dead are split between the Plymouth, Portsmouth, and Chatham Naval Memorials, organised by the men's home ports. The Plymouth memorial stands on the Hoe, the cliffs that families used to stand on to watch their ships head out to sea.

Scharnhorst limped into Trondheim with Glasfurd's hole in her side. She was repaired and sent back to sea. On 26 December 1943, off the North Cape of Norway, she was caught by HMS Duke of York in another running gunnery action and sunk. 1,932 Germans died. 36 survived.

The wreck of Scharnhorst was located in 2000. It lies upside down at 290 metres.
